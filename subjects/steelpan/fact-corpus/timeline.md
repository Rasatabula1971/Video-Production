# Verified timeline

Each entry is supportable if challenged in comments. Source: Series 1 doc §3.

| Period | Event |
|---|---|
| 1700s–1800s | Enslaved Africans in Trinidad; drumming central to Carnival and to communication. |
| 1884 | British colonial authorities ban African skin drums at Carnival, following the Canboulay Riots. |
| Late 1800s–early 1900s | Tamboo bamboo replaces drums — tuned bamboo lengths struck on the ground. |
| 1930s | Metal substitutes appear: biscuit tins, dustbin lids, car parts, paint tins. |
| Late 1930s | The decisive discovery — hammered indents on a metal surface produce distinct tuned pitches. |
| c. 1938–1940s | Early pans emerge; initially convex, later concave. |
| Post-1945 | 55-gallon oil drums discarded by refineries become the standard material. |
| 1945 | Steelbands appear in the streets of Port of Spain after the war's end. |
| 1951 | TASPO — the Trinidad All Steel Percussion Orchestra — performs at the Festival of Britain, London. First major international exposure. |
| 1992 | Steelpan declared the national instrument of Trinidad and Tobago. |

## Deeper background (from *Forged in Carnival*)

- Ancestry runs through 19th-century Trinidadian Carnival, Canboulay, African
  drumming traditions, and tamboo bamboo ensembles.
- By ~1939–1940, experimenters could produce distinguishable pitches on metal
  containers — the early melodic instrument called the **ping pong**.
- 1940s–50s pioneers turned pitched metal percussion into chromatic instruments:
  Winston "Spree" Simon, Ellie Mannette, Anthony Williams, Neville Jules,
  Sonny Roach and others. Later, Bertie Marshall and Rudolph Charles improved
  harmonic tuning, range, orchestration and construction.
- TASPO (1951) moved the instrument from street to concert stage; pans were
  built to concert pitch, increasingly from standard oil drums.
- The modern families followed: tenor, double tenor, double second, guitar,
  cello, bass.
- **Panorama** (from 1963) created the competitive environment that accelerated
  arranging, tuning and instrument development.
- Early development was centred in and around Port of Spain — East Dry River,
  John John, Laventille, Belmont, Newtown, Woodbrook, St James — in yards and
  streets, among young men with little formal engineering training but a deep
  empirical grasp of steel, vibration, resonance and pitch.
- Scholarship (e.g. Kim Johnson, *From Tin Pan to TASPO*) cautions against
  looking for a single "invention moment." The steelpan is a **Trinidadian
  innovation system** — players, tuners, builders and arrangers whose cumulative
  experimentation transformed industrial scrap into an instrument family.
